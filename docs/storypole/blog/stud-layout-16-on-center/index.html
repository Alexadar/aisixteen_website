How do you lay out studs 16 inches on center, and why does 19.2 exist?

By AISixteenPublished November 24, 20265 min read

Short answerHook the tape on the end of the plate, mark 15-1/4", then every 16" after that (31-1/4", 47-1/4"…), and set each stud just past its mark. That puts stud centres at 16", 32" and 48", so the edge of a 4-foot sheet lands on the middle of a stud. 19.2" is 96 ÷ 5: five bays per 8-foot sheet.

"On center" means the distance from the middle of one framing member to the middle of the next. The whole system exists so that 4 × 8 sheet goods always break on a stud. Once you see that, the odd numbers on a tape measure make sense.

Why 16 inches, and why is the first mark at 15-1/4?

A sheet of plywood, OSB or drywall is 48" × 96". Both divide evenly by 16 and by 24, so a sheet edge always falls on the centre line of a stud, with half the stud's thickness left for the next sheet.

A stud is 1-1/2" thick, so its centre is 3/4" in from either face. For the centre of a stud to sit exactly 16" from the end of the wall, its near edge must be at 16 − 3/4 = 15-1/4".

The layout, measured from the outside end of the plate:

  1. Mark 15-1/4". Draw a line and put an X on the far side of it. The stud covers the X.
  2. Mark every 16" after that: 31-1/4", 47-1/4", 63-1/4", 79-1/4", 95-1/4".
  3. Each stud's centre is now at 16", 32", 48", 64", 80" and 96".

Most tapes print the 16" multiples in red. Marking 3/4" back from each red number gives the same lines.

Lay out both plates together, clamped side by side, and always pull from the same end. Measuring stud to stud accumulates error; marks pulled from one end do not.

How many studs does a wall need?

Divide the wall length in inches by the spacing, round up, and add one for the starting end.

Example: a 14-foot wall (168") at 16" on center. 168 ÷ 16 = 10.5, round up to 11 spaces, so 12 studs. The last regular stud is centred at 160", leaving a final bay of only 8" to the end stud. That short bay is normal. Do not stretch the spacing to even it out, or the sheets stop landing on studs.

That count is the plain wall only. Corners, intersecting walls, and the king, jack and cripple studs around each opening are extra, which is why estimators often carry one stud per foot of wall as a quick allowance.

What are the black diamonds on a tape measure?

They mark multiples of 19.2": 19-3/16", 38-3/8", 57-5/8", 76-13/16" and 96". The number is 96 ÷ 5, which gives five equal bays under an 8-foot sheet instead of six (16") or four (24").

Spacing Bays per 8 ft Members per 8 ft run Used for
16" 6 7 Most walls and floors
19.2" 5 6 Engineered I-joists and floor trusses
24" 4 5 Roof trusses, advanced framing, some walls

19.2" saves one member in every 8 feet compared with 16", while an 8-foot sheet laid across the framing still ends on a member. The catch is the 4-foot direction: 48 ÷ 19.2 = 2.5, so a sheet's 4-foot edge does not land on a member. That is why 19.2" is used where sheathing runs with its long side across the joists. The spacing is a consequence of sheet size, not a figure taken from a table.

How do you space balusters or slats evenly?

Equal spacing is a different problem: you choose the number of pieces, and the gap is whatever makes them fit.

gap = (opening − number of pieces × piece width) ÷ (number of pieces + 1)

The residential code adds a limit for guards: a 4" sphere must not pass through (IRC R312.1.3). So the gap has to be under 4".

Worked example: a 72" opening between posts, balusters 1-1/2" wide.

  • With 12 balusters: (72 − 18) ÷ 13 = 4.15". Too wide.
  • With 13 balusters: (72 − 19.5) ÷ 14 = 3.75", or 3-3/4". That passes.

For the marks, the first baluster's near edge is one gap from the post: 3-3/4". Each next one is a gap plus a width further on: 5-1/4" later. So the near edges fall at 3-3/4", 9", 14-1/4", 19-1/2" and so on, pulled from one end.

What size is a 2x4 really?

1-1/2" × 3-1/2". "2 × 4" is the nominal size; the dressed, dry size is smaller, and it is standardised in the American Softwood Lumber Standard, NIST PS 20.

Nominal Dressed (dry)
2 × 4 1-1/2" × 3-1/2"
2 × 6 1-1/2" × 5-1/2"
2 × 8 1-1/2" × 7-1/4"
2 × 10 1-1/2" × 9-1/4"
2 × 12 1-1/2" × 11-1/4"
1 × 4 3/4" × 3-1/2"

Every layout number in this article depends on the 1-1/2" figure. With full-dimension or rough-sawn lumber, the 3/4" offset changes.

Where does Storypole fit?

Storypole is a job-site calculator for carpenters, framers and remodelers. Its layout tools give on-center marks at 16", 19.2" and 24" with the odd last bay stated, and equal spacing as the full list of marks to pull rather than only a count. It also does feet-inch-fraction tape math in exact fractions, board feet and dressed lumber sizes, roof pitch and rafters, miters and bevels, concrete in cubic yards and wire gauge. It names the standard behind each sourced value and labels 19.2" as derived (96 ÷ 5) rather than sourced. It runs offline on iPhone, iPad, Mac and Apple Watch.

It does not measure: there is no camera and no AR ruler. It does the math on a measurement you already took with a tape.

Frequently asked questions

Where do you start measuring for 16 on center?

From the outside end of the plate. Make the first mark at 15-1/4" and every 16" after it, with each stud set on the far side of its mark.

What is 19.2 inches on a tape measure?

19-3/16", marked with a black diamond. It is 96" divided by 5, giving five bays per 8-foot sheet.

How far apart can deck or stair balusters be?

Close enough that a 4" sphere cannot pass between them under the International Residential Code, so the clear gap must be less than 4". Check your local code.
